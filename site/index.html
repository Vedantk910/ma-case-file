How I built it.

The arc from a screening deck to a tool a sponsor can re-run on any deal.

  1. Frame one decision.“Which of these twelve deals do we buy?” reduced to three strategies priced on one yardstick — target-EBITDA units against the target's own DCF: buy, build in-house, partner in, or do nothing.
  2. Source only public numbers, each tagged.Twelve announced deal values from 8-Ks and 10-Ks (2015–2025), $0.33B to $16B, each with its source line. The EBITDA multiple is left un-invented — none of the twelve discloses a clean one, so the screen supplies its own multiples as decision inputs.
  3. Model with closed forms first.The buy-only boundary is one line of algebra, m*(c) = (E·conv + S·c) / (ds·r), rising 10x to 16.7x as capture rises 0 to 100%. The IRR waterfall is an additive gate bridge — equity in, growth, multiple expansion — whose implied IRR matches the bisection solver to 1e-3.
  4. Prove it, not just claim it.38 offline pytest cases: closed-form-vs-numeric pins, a scale-invariance check (deal value never moves the IRR), a decay-consistency check, and a seeded input-prior bootstrap so every headline number ships with P10/P50/P90. Zero network, deterministic, one-command re-run.
  5. Answer the hostile question.The IRR is decomposed, not defended: in the expansion case 82% of the gate work is the multiple re-rating, and growth-only strips it to 9%. The synergy-decay boundary — accretive 5 of 5 years at 8x, 0 of 5 at 18x — is the answer to “when do you stop buying?”
  6. Make it runnable and feelable.python -m notebooks.run_notebook regenerates out/decision_numbers.json; GitHub Actions runs the suite on every push; this page re-implements the same closed forms in JS and a Node harness pins the two languages to the same bits.

Three attacks, answered with a number.

What I'd get asked in a strategy interview — and the defence that holds.

Attack 1

“Your screen uses multiples that don't exist publicly — defend the decision inputs.”

They are decision inputs, not historical values: none of the twelve deals discloses a clean EBITDA multiple, so the screen supplies its own and the announced value sets capacity, not return — the IRR waterfall proves it, bit for bit, that a $16.0B deal and a $0.33B deal print identical gates at identical multiples.

Attack 2

“Is that IRR a real return, or a multiple assumption dressed up?”

The waterfall decomposes it: in the 6x-in/12x-out expansion case, 82% of the gate work is the multiple re-rating — strip it (growth-only, 8x in / 8x out) and the edge is 9%. The distribution is reported, not just the point estimate, and the gate split is a number the client can re-run.

Attack 3

“How do you know when to stop buying?”

The synergy-decay boundary: a deal is “accretive for N years, then inverts” — 5 of 5 at 8x, 3 of 5 at 14x, 0 of 5 at 18x — and the buy price cap is the point where the cheaper alternative overtakes: 12x for e-commerce, 16x for logistics. The trigger line a mandate quotes is a number, not a gut call.
